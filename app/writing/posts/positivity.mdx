# Positivity

“Does everyone agree?”

“Nope,” I promptly retorted.

Raj, our Cohort Director, was delivering the inaugural session of The Knowledge Society. And although I was glad to be there, I was strongly compelled to dissent. He’d just presented a diagram of careers in the modern global economy, complete with artists, teachers, researchers, writers, and more. Except it wasn’t just a diagram. It was a hierarchy. “We want to be here.” He pointed to the upper section, containing technology and finance founders, CEOs, and global diplomats and politicians. “These are the most powerful jobs in the world. These are the people that make an impact, the people that make history and have a bearing on our future. These are the most successful people, the positions that everyone aims for.”

I swiftly unmuted myself. “There’s nothing wrong with any of these other jobs on the list,” I argued. I saw a couple of subtle nods in agreement. “Some people want to center their lives on the work they do and the power they exert. Not only is it incorrect that these are indisputably the most powerful and influential people, but individuals seeking those things shouldn’t prioritize this mentality above all else—powerful careers are not everyone's path to fulfillment. It’s just as admirable to be someone who maybe isn’t wealthy or politically powerful, but devotes themselves and derives meaning from the work they do.” A couple of other students chimed in after me, and though Raj acknowledged my perspective, he jumped to defend himself. We ended up continuing the conversation after the brief speed bump, but I couldn’t help feeling unsettled.

When I first heard about The Knowledge Society, I was excited. “Join a global network of innovation-minded teenagers,” shouted the landing page. “We’re training the next generation of CEOs.” It sounded too good to be true, but I was curious. Seven essays, an interview, a video introduction, and an entire published article about Prime Editing (a recent development in CRISPR gene editing) later, I was accepted. Since they offered a scholarship, I found it hard to say no. Yet even before I began attending the weekly Zooms, I was skeptical if the program would deliver upon its promises. When it started, I sat back and made scrutinizing comments I thought were witty, disillusioned that a community centered around “unconventionality” seemed to prioritize such shallow goals as influence and fame.

Hardly two weeks into the program, my heart sank when I was called to meet with the international director. He expressed that I was making critical judgments without contributing to the community or completing the assigned projects. He asked a simple question. “Do you want to be in this program?”

During the call, I slowly realized I’d been behaving as a spectator, criticizing, rather than engaging in the community. I was enveloped in self-righteousness. Maybe my views were valid, but I was reacting, not responding. I was spending my time judging the program rather than delving into the content, taking action, and growing. I had chosen to be in this community, so it simply wasn’t productive to spend time participating with negativity. Though I still disagreed with some of the tenets presented, I certainly resonated with the overarching mission of the program.

I was initially hesitant to speak up in cohort conversations after hearing the feedback, but I gradually began to voice my opinion constructively. With a more buoyant spirit, I began to develop closer bonds with new friends in the vibrant, global TKS community, “nerding out,” as we like to call it, about everything from blockchain implementations to brain-computer interfaces. Since giving and receiving effective feedback was, in fact, one of the core goals of the program itself, I initiated and led a group of 16 peers in the cohort to give each other constructive feedback on our individual presentations and projects, developing our own guidelines for honest-yet-positive criticism that we used to accelerate one another’s growth. I eventually even worked with TKS’s marketing team to voice and carry through many of my ideas.

The experience transformed the way I participated in collaborative environments. At my educational nonprofit, I loosened my micromanaging grip over many of the organization’s core departments; when I encouraged our social media manager to do what she was best at, I was pleasantly surprised to see an Instagram feed better than anything I could have imagined. On the robotics team, I decided to comment less and build more, designing an introductory programming curriculum and empowering members to find their own solutions. I became an infinitely more supportive tutor, teacher, and leader. I prioritized relationships over concepts, curiosity over judgment, and commitment and action over critical outlook.

I’m never going to stop questioning. The chutzpah to critique a popular opinion and the ability to deeply analyze problems remain among my guiding principles. But beyond the curriculum itself, I’m incredibly grateful for the opportunity to grow that TKS granted me. In college, I’ll strive to voice ideas that no one quite considered. Though I have no doubt that I will find flaws, I’ve learned to guide collaboration by sowing curiosity rather than speculation and judgment. I’ll bring to the table a shifted perspective that positivity is just as, if not more, important to innovation than criticism.
